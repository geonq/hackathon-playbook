# Event-Day Rules and Idea Strategy

Use this guide to decide what the team may build, then compare eligible ideas. It is an internal preparation method, not an organizer rule, official judging rubric, or prediction of results.

## 1. Clear the rules and eligibility gate

Before locking an idea or starting event-related work, record the controlling rule source and its date: organizer briefing, official event page, attendee email, or written answer from the host. Separate confirmed rules from questions still open.

For the event and every award or challenge the team may target, confirm:

- who may participate, team-size limits, and any separate registration or submission;
- the track or challenge the idea is eligible for, including eligibility for a sponsor challenge;
- required technology and the role it must play, if any;
- allowed pre-event work, code, prompts, data, models, and assets;
- approved data and services, privacy limits, and any human-review requirement;
- required deliverables, submission method, deadline, timezone, and demo format;
- code, data, asset, license, publication, and post-event reuse terms.

Treat an unanswered item as unresolved. Do not lock an idea or claim eligibility that depends on an unresolved rule. Ask the organizer and update the plan when the answer arrives. Event descriptions and sponsor language do not replace the controlling rules.

If events run on consecutive days, keep their projects and materials separate. Reuse Saturday code, data, prompts, assets, or a project on Sunday only when both events' rules or organizers explicitly permit it; disclose reuse when required. Participation in one event does not grant permission for work at another.

Use the [competition plan template](../templates/competition-plan.md) to record sources, eligibility, owners, and provenance.

## 2. Compare only eligible ideas

If organizers provide a judging rubric, copy its exact criteria and weights into the [competition plan](../templates/competition-plan.md), then map each criterion to visible evidence and a demo moment. Use that confirmed rubric to steer scope and presentation. If no rubric is published, record that fact; the scorecard below remains an internal planning aid, not a substitute or prediction of judging.

After eligibility passes, shortlist no more than three ideas. Use this internal scorecard to compare them; higher scores mean a stronger fit for this team's one-day build, not a higher expected judging score.

Score each factor from 1 to 5:

| Factor | A high score means |
|---|---|
| User value | A specific person has a real, understandable task or pain point. |
| Evidence obtainable today | The team can test the claimed outcome with approved examples and a clear baseline before the deadline. |
| Execution risk | The critical path is small, dependencies are available, and likely failures have a workable response. |
| Meaningful technical contribution | The required or selected technology performs a necessary, visible part of the workflow. |
| Explainability | The team can show what happened, what evidence supports the claim, and what the prototype cannot do. |

Write one reason and one major unknown beside each score. Prefer an idea that passes every relevant eligibility gate, has a complete user path, and can produce honest evidence within the event clock. Re-score it if the rules, available tools, or problem-owner input changes.

## 3. Commit to a demonstrable plan

For the selected idea, name the user, workflow, approved input, baseline, evaluation cases, owners, feature cuts, freeze time, safe submission cutoff, and limitations. Tie each official criterion to a concrete result, evidence artifact, and demo moment when a rubric exists. Get the thinnest complete path running early. Protect the final 60–90 minutes for testing, stabilization, submission, and rehearsal when the confirmed schedule allows.

Tell the demo as user problem → result → necessary role of the technology → observed evidence → limits. Do not present this structure or scorecard as an official rubric.
